## §1 — Why Companies Decide to Enter Foreign Markets — pp. 179–180 (PDF 222–223) [LO1]

Five major reasons (numbered in chapter):
1. **Gain access to new customers** — revenue/profit/growth; extend product life cycle (Honda 50-cc Cub); faster payback on large investments in R&D-intensive industries.
2. **Achieve lower costs** through economies of scale, experience/learning-curve, and increased (pooled) purchasing power — small home markets force expansion (Michelin, BMW, Nestlé).
3. **Gain access to low-cost inputs of production** — natural-resource industries; low-cost labor where labor is a high share of cost.
4. **Further exploit core competencies** — extend domestic leading position regionally/globally (H&M online retailing); replicate a successful business model (Starbucks, McDonald's).
5. **Gain access to resources and capabilities located in foreign markets** — cross-border acquisitions/alliances to learn or complement; local distribution networks, managerial/marketing expertise, technical knowledge.
Plus: **suppliers follow major customers abroad** (automotive parts suppliers; Newell-Rubbermaid following Walmart).

## §2 — Why Competing Across National Borders Makes Strategy Making More Complex — pp. 181–188 (PDF 224–231) [LO2]

Five complexity drivers (chapter intro list, p. 181):
(1) home-country advantages differ by country/industry; (2) location-based advantages for value chain activities; (3) political/economic-climate differences; (4) currency-exchange-rate risk; (5) buyer-taste differences → customization-vs-standardization tension.

### 2a. Home-Country Industry Advantages and the Diamond Model — pp. 181–183 (PDF 224–226) [FIGURE 7.1]
- Porter's **Diamond of National Competitive Advantage** (Figure 7.1, book p. 182 / PDF 225). Four factors:
  - **Demand conditions** — home-market size/growth, buyer needs; demanding buyers spur innovation (China autos example).
  - **Factor conditions** — availability/quality/cost of inputs: labor, knowledge, land, capital, natural resources, infrastructure (India vs China distribution example).
  - **Related and supporting industries** — clusters (Italian "engine technological district": Ferrari, Maserati, Ducati).
  - **Firm strategy, structure, and rivalry** — management styles (alliances in Asia/LatAm vs N.A. individualism); fierce home rivalry hones capability.
- All four must be favorable for an industry to be internationally strong.
- Three managerial uses of the diamond: predict where foreign entrants come from; reveal where foreign rivals are weakest (which markets to enter first); guide where to locate value chain activities.

### 2b. Opportunities for Location-Based Advantages — pp. 183–184 (PDF 226–227)
- Manufacturing-cost variation: wage rates, productivity, energy, regulation, supplier proximity, natural resources; low-cost countries become export production havens (2013 hourly mfg compensation comparison: India $1.46 … Norway $65.86; China's rise and wage doubling).
- Quality/availability considerations for other activities: Tiffany conflict-free diamonds (Canada); call centers in India/Ireland; R&D near prestigious institutions; distribution near markets.

### 2c. Impact of Government Policies and Economic Conditions in Host Countries — pp. 184–185 (PDF 227–228)
- Pro-business climates: tax incentives, low-cost loans, site assistance, training (Ireland).
- Unattractive policies: environmental-compliance costs, subsidies to domestic firms, burdensome customs, tariffs/quotas, local-content rules, capital controls, partial local-ownership requirements, export restrictions, price regulation.
- **CORE CONCEPT — Political risks** (weak/unstable governments, hostility to foreign business, nationalization/expropriation — Argentina/YPF/Repsol 2012; war, unrest, terrorism, corruption) vs **economic risks** (monetary-system instability, inflation, deficit/lending breakdowns, IP piracy/weak property rights, currency fluctuation).

### 2d. The Risks of Adverse Exchange Rate Shifts — pp. 185–187 (PDF 228–230)
- Rates can move 20–40%/yr; risky because (1) hard to predict, (2) create uncertainty over which country is the low-cost location.
- Worked example: Brazilian-real plant exporting to EU at 4 → 5 reals/euro (real weakens: cost falls 1 € → 0.8 €, more competitive + lower euro price boosts exports) vs 4 → 3 (real strengthens: 1.33 €, weaker position).
- Rule: **weaker home/producer currency = favorable shift for exporters**; four bullet effects of a weaker US dollar (raises dollar cost of foreign rivals' goods, curbs US imports, boosts US exports, increases dollar value of foreign profits); strong dollar reverses; same logic for the euro.
- Margin note: exporters disadvantaged when manufacturing-country currency strengthens vs importing-country currency; domestic firms facing imports benefit from weaker home currency.

### 2e. Cross-Country Differences in Demographic, Cultural, and Market Conditions — pp. 187–188 (PDF 230–231)
- Taste examples: French top-loading washers; HK compact vs Taiwan large appliances; East-Asian novelty ice cream; 110 V vs 240 V standards; South Korea PC reluctance.
- The central tension: **localize (responsiveness, appeal) vs standardize (scale economies, learning-curve, low cost)** — "one of the big strategic issues" → bridge to §4.

## §3 — Strategic Options for Entering International Markets — pp. 188–193 (PDF 231–236) [LO3]

Five options (listed p. 188); choice depends on strategic objectives, resources/capabilities, country factors (trade barriers), transaction costs; **higher investment & risk ↔ greater ownership & control**.

### 3a. Export strategies — pp. 188–189
- Conservative initial entry; minimal capital; use foreign wholesalers or own distribution; favored by Chinese/Korean/Italian firms; captures centralized scale/learning economies.
- Vulnerable when: (1) home manufacturing costs higher than rivals' plants abroad, (2) high shipping costs, (3) adverse exchange shifts, (4) tariffs/trade barriers.

### 3b. Licensing strategies — p. 189
- Fits firms with technical know-how/brand/patent but no internal capability/resources to go abroad; royalty income, shifts cost/risk to licensee; avoids risky-market commitments.
- Big disadvantage: risk to proprietary know-how, loss of control, hard monitoring. Used by software & pharma.

### 3c. Franchising strategies — pp. 189–190
- Better for service/retail (McDonald's, Yum! Brands, UPS Store, Roto-Rooter, 7-Eleven, Hilton); franchisee bears costs/risks; franchisor only recruits/trains/supports/monitors.
- Problem: quality control across cultures; how much local menu/product adaptation to allow (McDonald's, KFC China questions).

### 3d. Foreign subsidiary strategies — pp. 190–191
- Wholly owned for full control: **acquisition** vs **greenfield/internal startup** (CORE CONCEPT, p. 190).
- Acquisition: quicker; least risky/cost-efficient way to hurdle entry barriers (distribution access, supplier & government relationships); resource/personnel transfer; premium-successful-firm vs bargain-struggling-firm decision.
- Greenfield: appealing when company already multi-country experienced, with deep resources; four added conditions (cheaper than acquisition; capacity won't upset local supply–demand; good distribution access; size/cost/capability to fight local rivals).
- Greenfield drawbacks: costly capital, high risk, resource diversion, weak where institutions/markets weak, **slowest entry route** (but potentially higher returns).

### 3e. Alliance and joint venture strategies — pp. 191–193 [Illustration Capsule 7.1]
- Six benefits: local partner's market/regulatory knowledge; scale economies in production/marketing; shared distribution/dealer access; learning (joint research, know-how, local marketing); directing competitive energy at mutual rivals; agreeing technical standards.
- vs M&A: preserves independence, conserves capital, flexible disengagement; can pave way for intended merger (test before commitment) — **IC 7.1 Walgreens + Alliance Boots → Walgreens Boots Alliance (2014)**, pp. 191–192.
- **Risks** (pp. 191–193): partner knowledge less valuable than expected/obsolete; language & cultural barriers; conflicting practices/objectives/values; high transaction & coordination costs; loss of competitive advantage if partner gains access to proprietary capabilities; suspicion limits exchange; over-dependence on partner for essential expertise — global leaders must develop own capabilities; experienced multinationals need alliances less (hire local managers instead).
- Lesson: alliances better for establishing a **beachhead** than for sustaining global leadership.

## §4 — International Strategy: The Three Main Approaches — pp. 193–198 (PDF 236–241) [LO4; FIGURE 7.2; TABLE 7.1; IC 7.2]

- **CORE CONCEPT: international strategy** = strategy for competing in two or more countries simultaneously (p. 193).
- The central trade-off: **local responsiveness vs global integration/standardization efficiency** — Figure 7.2 (book p. 194 / PDF 237) positions the three options on these two axes.

### 4a. Multidomestic — think-local, act-local — pp. 194–195
- CORE CONCEPT def.; varies product & competitive approach country by country; decentralized decision making. Examples: Castrol 3,000 formulas; food-products localization; BP/Arco/Aral brands.
- Fits: high need for local responsiveness + limited standardization gains.
- Three drawbacks: hinders cross-border resource/capability transfer (less innovative); higher production & distribution costs; not conducive to a single worldwide competitive advantage (only varied local edges).

### 4b. Global — think-global, act-global — pp. 195–196
- CORE CONCEPT def.; standardized, globally integrated; same products/brands/channels/capabilities everywhere; centralized value chain, strong HQ control; minor local adaptation only.
- Fits: homogeneous needs + pronounced standardization efficiencies (scale, learning, replication, R&D fixed-cost spreading; global competition conditions). Example: Ford global design (Fiesta 2010, Focus 2011). Preference rule: be as global as conditions permit (unified operations, uniform brand image, full resource focus).
- Four drawbacks: can't address local needs precisely; less responsive to local changes; higher transport costs/tariffs; higher coordination costs.

### 4c. Transnational — think-global, act-local ("glocalization") — pp. 196–197
- CORE CONCEPT def.; middle ground when both responsiveness needs and standardization benefits are high; mass-customization techniques (McDonald's, KFC, Starbucks menus; Unilever; Otis multi-brand China — share doubled, revenue 6× in nine years; EA dispersed design studios).
- Most conducive to transferring/leveraging subsidiary skills; three drawbacks: hardest to implement (complexity); conflicting objectives; costly/time-consuming with uncertain outcome.
- **TABLE 7.1** (book p. 197 / PDF 240): advantages/disadvantages of all three approaches — must be reproduced and read through.
- **IC 7.2 Four Seasons Hotels: Local Character, Global Service** (book pp. 197–198 / PDF 240–241): local capital partner, local architects, unique layouts (Mumbai banquet halls, Middle East prayer rooms, Bali weather shaman), local + "culture carrier" staffing, global service systems — exemplary transnational strategy vs Ritz-Carlton's uniform-global approach.

## §5 — International Operations and the Quest for Competitive Advantage — pp. 199–202 (PDF 242–245) [LO5]

Three routes to advantage from international operations (p. 199):

### 5a. Using location — pp. 199–200
Two decisions: concentrate vs disperse each activity; which countries.
- **Concentrate few locations when:** costs significantly lower there (Asian footwear, Taiwan circuit boards); significant scale economies (digital cameras/LED TVs — Japan/Korea/Taiwan; largest worldwide manufacturing share; making rivals' branded units; regional mega distribution centers); strong learning/experience effects (cumulative volume); superior resources/coordination (Samsung Silicon Valley R&D; JIT supplier clustering; customer-service proximity).
- **Disperse to many locations when:** buyer-related activities must be near buyers (distribution, marketing, after-sale service — mining/oil-drilling equipment, accounting firms); high transport costs/diseconomies of size/trade barriers; hedging exchange-rate, supply-interruption, political risks. Non-buyer activities (procurement, parts, assembly, R&D, new-product development, capital raising) can be decoupled and located wherever advantage lies.

### 5b. Sharing and transferring resources & capabilities across borders — pp. 200–202
- Brand-name leverage (Hermes, Prada, Gucci); know-how transfer (Whirlpool's 70-site IT platform; Walmart's distribution capability into 28 countries).
- Economics: low cost of sharing vs creating; spreads fixed development costs → low unit costs (Disney theme-park replication walkthrough).
- Builds deeper competencies via larger domain, tougher rivals, learning (Honda quality reputation: motorcycles → autos → power equipment); one-country base often too small.
- **Caveats — not a guaranteed recipe:** advantage depends on local rivalry conditions; resources valuable in one country may not transfer (Philips/Magnavox North America losses since 1988).

### 5c. Benefiting from cross-border coordination — p. 202
- Shift production between plants (exchange rates, shortages, wage/energy changes); coordinate worldwide schedules and divert shipments; leverage with host governments; respond to tariff/quota changes; shift workloads to underutilized locations.

## §6 — Cross-Border Strategic Moves — pp. 202–204 (PDF 245–247)

- **CORE CONCEPT: profit sanctuaries** = country markets yielding substantial profits due to strong/protected position; home market usually the biggest; global firms have several (Nike: North America + Greater China, 2015 revenues $13.7B / $3.1B); domestic-only firms have one.
- **Offensive use:** financial strength for offensives elsewhere; lowball pricing/heavy marketing in domestic rival's home market; **CORE CONCEPT: cross-market subsidization** — supporting offensives in one market with profits from another; speed-adjustable inroads; retaliation by the domestic firm exposes its whole profit base.
- **Dumping** definition (prices below home-market prices or below full unit costs); government retaliation via special tariffs; **WTO** policing; pressure from own government.
- **Defensive use:** counterattack the rival in its key market where it has most to lose; presence in a rival's key markets + a sanctuary = deterrence signal (capacity to fund counterattack); **mutual restraint** among multimarket rivals stabilizes competition (CORE CONCEPT margin note).

## §7 — Strategies for Competing in the Markets of Developing Countries — pp. 204–206 (PDF 247–249) [LO6]

- Stakes: BRIC + Indonesia, Thailand, Poland, Mexico; huge growth, considerable risk; China #2 economy, 5th-largest luxury market.
- Tailoring goes beyond minor changes (McDonald's veggie burgers/pricing; Kellogg cereal habits; single-serve packets in India).
- Four strategy options:
  1. **Compete on low price** (Unilever Wheel detergent India — low-cost product, plant, single-use packets, handcart distribution, economical marketing → $100M, #1 brand; replicated with shampoos/deodorants and Ala in South America).
  2. **Modify the business model to local circumstances** (Honeywell "East for East" — local engineers/strategists in China & India → 30% of worldwide growth).
  3. **Change the local market** (Suzuki India quality revolution in auto parts; Mahindra & Mahindra J.D. Power #1).
  4. **Stay away where adaptation is impractical/uneconomical** (Home Depot: Mexico yes, China exit 2012 — value proposition depends on highways/logistics, employee stock ownership, high labor costs that favor DIY).
- Margin note: profitability rarely quick/easy — consumer reeducation, advertising investment, infrastructure upgrades; patience required.

## §8 — Defending Against Global Giants: Strategies for Local Companies in Developing Countries — pp. 206–208 (PDF 249–251) [LO6; IC 7.3]

Five proven defensive strategies (Khanna & Palepu research base):
1. **Business models exploiting shortcomings in local distribution/infrastructure** (Shanda prepaid game cards via merchants; India all-in-one business machine tolerant of power outages).
2. **Keen understanding of local needs → customized products/services**; accept losing global-brand-seeking customers (Muslim-oriented cell phone; Tencent instant messaging).
3. **Exploit local workforce characteristics** unfamiliar to multinationals (Focus Media bicycle-courier display updates; Infosys/Satyam second-tier-city recruiting vs EDS/Accenture).
4. **Acquisition and rapid-growth strategies** to build scale first nationally then internationally (Hindalco; Lenovo–IBM PC).
5. **Transfer expertise cross-border and contend internationally** (Televisa Spanish-language soap operas; Sundaram Fasteners GM radiator caps → QS 9000 → Japan/Europe).
- **IC 7.3 Ctrip** (book pp. 207–208 / PDF 250–251): tailored business model (call center + proprietary database of 5,600 hotels, couriers collecting payments by bicycle/scooter, loyalty program), low-cost labor, local preferences → 60% of China online travel by 2011; Qunar mobile competition → 2013 cross-selling partnership, 2015 share exchange, >80% combined of China hotel & air-ticket markets.
